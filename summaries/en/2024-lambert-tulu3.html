SECTION 01

Problem — Open Source Lacks the Recipe, Not the Model

By late 2024 the open base models (Llama 3.1, Qwen 2.5) were strong. The pipeline that turns a base model into a genuinely useful chat model, however, remained private to a handful of labs.

The paper's opening diagnosis is blunt: post-training — the collection of techniques including instruction tuning, RLHF, and other finetuning — has become a crucial step in building frontier models, yet advances in these techniques are frequently not accompanied by open resources and recipes. A footnote nails the point: on LMSYS ChatBot Arena as of November 20, 2024, not one model in the top 50 had released its post-training data.

Fully open-source counterparts do exist — Tülu 2, Zephyr-β — but they rely on simpler, cheaper pipelines and have fallen behind on core capabilities like MATH, IFEval, and GSM8K. They still look reasonable on chat evaluations such as AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard, which masks the real capability gap.

What Tülu 3 actually is: not just a model, but a reproducible complete recipe. Four artifact families are released: Tülu 3 Data (new, permissively licensed training datasets), Tülu 3 Eval (evaluation suite plus decontamination tooling), Tülu 3 Code (training and infrastructure code), and Tülu 3 Recipe (the multi-stage pipeline with every hyperparameter). Intermediate checkpoints are released too.

Five headline contributions

01 · Evaluation and decontamination tooling

A dev / unseen split evaluation suite plus an 8-gram matching decontamination toolchain — and a public accounting of which popular open datasets are already contaminated against which benchmarks.

02 · Scaled synthetic instruction datasets

250K personas from Persona Hub drive GPT-4o to generate math, code, and precise-IF data, sidestepping the mode collapse typical of synthetic generation.

03 · Scaling preference data with on-policy generations

An extension of the UltraFeedback pipeline that mixes in completions from Tülu 3 SFT itself, yielding 354,192 preference instances.

04 · RLVR

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards — replace the reward model with a verification function; reward only when the answer is verified correct.

05 · Infrastructure for large models

Asynchronous RL (vLLM inference concurrent with learner gradient updates), cached DPO log probs, Ray + PagedAttention GPU allocation — all the way to 405B.

＋ · Negative results

A dedicated section on what did not work: Online DPO and rejection sampling. Rare in a technical report.

SECTION 02

The Recipe — Four Stages

Tülu 3 starts from Llama 3.1 base models and runs four stages. Each stage builds on the previous model and focuses on a different type of data: prompt-completion, preference pairs, verifiable rewards.

Figure 1 · The Tülu 3 four-stage recipe (redrawn from paper Figure 1, with per-stage data volumes added)

Stage by stage

Data Curation (§3)

Curate prompts to be allocated across the later stages. Source from public datasets where they exist (tracking provenance and licenses); synthesize where they don't. Decontaminate everything against Tülu 3 Eval.

Supervised Finetuning (§4)

SFT on carefully selected prompt-completion pairs. Extensive experimentation determines the final mix and hyperparameters, with the goal of improving target skills without significantly hurting others.

Preference Tuning (§5)

DPO on newly curated preference data — on-policy completions sampled from Tülu 3-SFT itself, plus off-policy data from other models.

RL with Verifiable Rewards (§6)

A new final stage. Pick tasks with ground-truth outcomes (mathematics, verifiable instruction following), reward only when a generation is verified correct, and maximize that reward with PPO.

An easily overlooked design choice: all four stages share the same Tülu 3 Eval development suite as a compass. The methodology is not "run the pipeline once and look at the results" — it is first build single-skill upper-bound models (e.g. a math-specialized model), then mix data to push the generalist model toward that upper bound. This turns "how much headroom does skill X still have" into a measurable question.

SECTION 03

Data Curation — Where 23.3M Prompts Come From

Prompts are the common starting point for every post-training stage. Tülu 3 curates roughly 23.3M prompts, then selects 939K for SFT and 425K for DPO from that pool.

Three selection principles

Diversity

WildChat (real user interactions), Open Assistant (volunteer-annotated), No Robots (expert-annotated), FLAN v2 (a large compilation of classical NLP tasks).

Target skills

OpenMathInstruct 2 / NuminaMath (math), Evol-CodeAlpaca (code), Aya (multilingual), SciRIFF (scientific literature), TableGPT (table tasks).

Provenance & license

Only datasets with clear, correct licenses. ShareGPT is excluded entirely (users never agreed to training use) in favor of WildChat; Helpsteer2 was dropped because its prompts use ShareGPT.

Persona-driven synthesis: how to dodge mode collapse

The core problem with synthetic data is that LMs fall into repetitive patterns — mode collapse. Tülu 3 follows the persona-driven methodology of Chan et al. (2024): condition generation on a persona, e.g. "a machine learning researcher focused on neural networks," paired with a data synthesis prompt like "create a coding problem," steering the LLM toward that perspective. Concretely they condition on 250K personas from Persona Hub and generate with GPT-4o-2024-08-06.

These are the actual prompts from the paper's appendix, not paraphrases:

Per-skill synthesis details:

· Precise IF: covers the 25 constraint types defined in IFEval. The authors hand-wrote 1–2 examples per constraint (33 verifiable instructions total) as seeds, then generated new instructions with GPT-4o, ending with 29,980 verifiable instruction-response pairs (called If-Persona-Sft).

· Math & Coding: GPT-4o is zero-shot prompted to generate problems; multi-step math solutions come from GPT-4o, while Python programs are generated with claude-3-5-sonnet. 220K math reasoning and 35K coding instances in total.

· Noncompliance & Safety: built on the contextual noncompliance taxonomy of Brahman et al. (2024) — incomplete, unsupported, indeterminate, and humanizing requests — plus synthetic adversarial prompts and in-the-wild interactions.

Decontamination: 8-gram matching

The authors tried full-string, n-gram, and embedding-based matching. N-gram won. Embedding methods can in principle catch paraphrase-style contamination, but in practice it proved hard to distinguish mere distributional similarity from actual paraphrasing; partial surface-level overlap via n-grams, meanwhile, successfully caught the "same math problem with different numbers" case.

Decision rule:
· Compute overlap on prompts / user turns only (completions are often regenerated by LMs)
· A test token matches if its instance shares an 8-gram containing that token with a train instance
· A test instance has significant overlap if > 50% of its tokens have 8-gram matches with the same train instance
· A training set is contaminated if any of its instances overlap with > 2% of instances in any evaluation

Remediation is asymmetric: every training set contaminated against the unseen suite is removed outright. For sets contaminated against the development suite, the whole dataset is removed if that does not significantly hurt the resulting model; otherwise only the matching instances are dropped.

SECTION 04

Supervised Finetuning — The Engineering of Data Mixing

The core challenge in the SFT stage is not the algorithm but the mixing proportions: how to allocate data across skills without robbing Peter to pay Paul.

From prompts to SFT data

Two routes to responses. Filter existing ones: keep the original if it was written by a human or a frontier model like GPT-4o; for large datasets with multiple subsets (e.g. WildChat), take only the best-model subset. Generate new ones: Persona prompts have no responses, and some original responses come from weaker models (e.g. WildGuardMix), so those are regenerated with GPT-4o. Empty responses and responses containing information about models or their developers are filtered out.

The mixing process

Start with "Llama 3.1 + Tülu 2 mix" as the baseline and identify which skills lag SOTA. For each skill, build a skill-specific mixture and model in isolation, keeping the mixture that maximizes that one skill while ignoring the others — this approximates the upper bound for that evaluation under this setup. Then combine those mixtures into an initial Tülu 3 preview mix and iterate: add and remove datasets to lift lagging skills, decontaminate, and downsample the very large datasets.

The batch aggregation loss bug (§4.3.2) — worth reading in full.

Early on, SFT models trained in the authors' Open-Instruct framework underperformed the same recipe trained on TPUs. The cause was a loss aggregation issue in Transformers: averaging loss across padding tokens without accounting for gradient accumulation or distributed training.

Take a batch of two samples with n₁, n₂ non-padding tokens. Passing both through the default forward pass gives L = (l_n1 + l_n2) / (n1 + n2) — every token weighted equally. But with gradient accumulation, feeding them separately and then dividing gives L = (l_n1/n1 + l_n2/n2) / 2 — every example weighted equally. So changing gradient accumulation silently changes sample weighting and can substantially change performance. Cross-device averaging in distributed training has the same problem.

Tülu 3's fix is direct: use a sum loss instead of a mean loss, removing the denominator so all tokens are weighted equally (with a corresponding learning-rate adjustment). Validated by finetuning Llama 3.0 on the Tülu 2 SFT mixture across learning rates, epochs, and loss types: sum loss + LR 5e-6 worked best, and — surprisingly — training longer did not help, hence 2 epochs.

Three smaller findings

  • The base model matters a lot (Table 12). Same mix: Llama 3.1 8B → GSM8K 76.2 / MATH 31.5; 70B → 91.1 / 53.7; Qwen 2.5 7B → 79.2 / 49.4; Qwen 2.5 Math 7B → 86.3 / 56.4. Both model size and domain-specific pretraining data propagate directly into downstream skills.
  • Chat template (Table 13). Replacing the trailing newline in assistant messages with an eos token scored best (53.0), but the authors chose the simpler "just remove the newline" Tülu 3 template (52.8) to avoid generation inconsistency later in the pipeline. A deliberate trade.
  • Random seeds and model soups (Table 14). SFT performance varies noticeably with seed (8B: 59.8–60.1; 70B: 70.0–71.8), and the best model soup does not always beat the best single run (70B soup 72.5 vs best single 72.6). So the final models use the best single SFT run.
SECTION 05

Preference Tuning — On-Policy Data + Length-Normalized DPO

Two protagonists in this section: a synthetic pipeline that can mass-produce preference data, and an algorithm variant selected by experiment rather than fashion.

The math, stated plainly

In the standard setup, a preference dataset D contains prompts x and two responses; a judge picks the chosen y_c and labels the other rejected y_r. The reward model objective is:

maxr   E(x,yc,yr)∼D [ log σ( r(x,yc) − r(x,yr) ) ]
σ is the logistic function; the difference represents the log-likelihood that y_c is preferred over y_r

The policy objective — what PPO and other online RL methods optimize directly — is:

maxπ   Ey∼π(x) [ R(x,y) ] = [ r(x,y) − β · KL[ π(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x) ] ]
π_ref is the initial reference policy; β controls the KL divergence between reference and training policy

DPO shows an equivalent objective can be optimized directly — no reward model to train, no policy generations to sample:

maxπ   E(yc,yr)∼D [ log σ( β log π(yc|x) / πref(yc|x) − β log π(yr|x) / πref(yr|x) ) ]

Tülu 3 settles on length-normalized DPO — divide each log-ratio by the length of the corresponding response:

maxπ   E(yc,yr)∼D [ log σ( β/|yc| · log π(yc|x)/πref(yc|x) − β/|yr| · log π(yr|x)/πref(yr|x) ) ]
Intuition: mitigates the length bias common in human and model preferences (Singhal et al., 2024)

Seven ablation findings

✓ More unique prompts is better

Holding preference dataset size fixed while increasing unique prompts produces noticeable gains across several metrics. This is why the final mixes exceed 270K / 330K instances.

✗ Duplicating prompts is not

Expanding UltraFeedback by pairing its four responses into 64k / 180k / 383k instances (all with the same 64k unique prompts): 383k performs about the same as 64k, and DROP, GSM8K, and AlpacaEval degrade slightly as duplication grows.

✓ Unused prompts edge out reused ones

Prompts from the same sources that SFT did not consume slightly outperform reusing SFT prompts. But the best mix uses both.

✓ On-policy data helps

Adding responses generated by Tülu 3 SFT itself (one on-policy, one off-policy per pair) improves aggregate performance over a strictly off-policy dataset.

≈ LLM judges barely differ

GPT-4o 57.3, Llama 3.1 405B 57.2, GPT-4 Turbo 57.0, GPT-4o Mini 56.9, Llama 3.1 70B 56.6. The gaps are small enough that the paper bolds nothing. GPT-4o was chosen for ease of use, cost per request, and Batch API speed.

✓ Beyond UltraFeedback

The best mix beats UltraFeedback alone: +1.8 at 8B, +3.3 at 70B. The larger gap at 70B is hypothesized to be because UltraFeedback's completions come mostly from models weaker than the 70B starting point.

△ Only Persona IF works for preferences

Of the three Persona preference datasets, only Persona IF improves both the average and its target IFEval score. Persona Math and Persona Code fail to improve their own target evaluations and slightly hurt the average. Only Persona IF makes the final mix.

✓ Regenerating completions improves datasets

Taking prompts from Helpsteer2, UltraFeedback, and MultiPref and regenerating completions and preference annotations through Tülu 3's pipeline beats the original datasets downstream — the pipeline itself is a source of gain.

The counterintuitive result worth sitting with: Persona Math / Code data is clearly effective at the SFT stage (removing it costs 8 points of MATH and 12 of GSM8K), yet the same persona method fails entirely when moved to preference data. This suggests "which data helps at which stage" does not transfer by analogy — SFT teaches format and capability, preference tuning teaches ranking, and the two need different kinds of data.

PPO vs DPO: why DPO for the preference stage

Later in development the authors ran a deeper controlled comparison: train an RM on the same preference mixture (taking only the RM's logits at the EOS token as the reward, with the linear head initialized from N(0, 1/√(d_model+1))), then run PPO on the same prompts against DPO.

Result 1 · comparable scores

In this non-tuned setup PPO reached a comparable — albeit slightly lower — average than DPO.

Result 2 · 7× the cost

PPO: ~28 hours on two nodes. DPO: ~4 hours on one node. Combined with the subtlety of RM evaluation (strong RM benchmark performance does not imply better downstream PPO), the authors reserved PPO for RLVR.

Two optimizations to scale DPO to 70B

  1. Cache DPO log probs — precompute and cache log probabilities across the dataset using the initial model, so no GPU memory is allocated for a reference model during training.
  2. Separate forward passes for chosen and rejected — the canonical implementation concatenates them, effectively doubling batch size. Running two separate forward passes saves the memory.

Both were validated on Llama 3.1 with near-identical training losses and a clear reduction in peak GPU memory.

SECTION 06

RLVR — The Paper's Central Contribution

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards: keep the RLHF objective, but swap the learned reward model for a deterministic verification function. Correct answer, reward. Otherwise, zero.

Figure 3 · How RLVR works (redrawn from paper Figure 18 with key implementation details added)

The objective

maxπ   Ey∼π(x) [ RRLVR(x,y) ] = [ v(x,y) − β · KL[ π(y|x) ‖ πref(y|x) ] ]

where   v(x,y) = α  if the answer is correct
               = 0  otherwise

α = 10, set from pilot experiments and never tuned further · optimized with PPO · differs from the standard KL-constrained RLHF objective only in replacing the learned RM with v

The paper's own positioning is honest: RLVR can be seen as a simplified form of existing LM reasoning bootstrapping methods (STaR and relatives), or a simpler form of RL with execution feedback — just using answer matching or constraint verification as a binary signal. Prior work had already applied something similar to improve math alone (Kazemnejad et al., 2024); Tülu 3's increment is extending it across multiple evaluations and integrating it into a generalist training pipeline.

Five PPO implementation details (copy these)

Initialize the value model from a general RM

Not from the policy. This choice wins clearly in ablations (below).

Disable dropout

Set dropout to 0 during RM and RL training. The reasoning is precise: PPO computes token log probs twice — during rollout and during learning. If dropout makes them disagree, the ratio in the first PPO epoch is not 1, and every ratio may get clipped, zeroing the gradient.

Shuffle between epochs

PPO can train for more episodes than available prompts (the ablations run ~100,000/7,473 ≈ 13 epochs), so shuffle in between. Final runs checkpoint every 40–100 steps and pick the best on the development suite.

−10 penalty for non-EOS responses

PPO samples a fixed maximum number of tokens; a response that does not end with EOS gets a −10 penalty, encouraging the model to finish its answers.

Advantage whitening

Subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. Standard practice.

Four key findings

✓ RLVR does improve targeted domains

Training separately on GSM8K, MATH, and IF, the verifiable reward (train-set correctness) rises consistently in all three, and the corresponding test scores rise too. But note: an increase in the overall average is not guaranteed. Interestingly, on GSM8K and MATH, spending more KL budget does not necessarily buy higher verifiable rewards.

✓ Value model from a general RM works best

Against initializing from the anchored DPO model, a general RM initialization gets both the highest GSM8K test score and the higher average — suggesting the value function plays an important role in RLVR.

✗ Do not add the RM score

A plausible implementation is "verifiable reward + RM score." In practice verifiable rewards alone perform better; adding the RM score introduces noise, especially visible in the average.

△ Starting weaker converges to the same reward

Starting from either SFT or DPO reaches the same level of verifiable reward, but at the same β starting from SFT incurs a larger KL (it is further from being good at GSM8K). Starting from a stronger model still yields better test performance.

Overoptimization happens — and it looks bad. The lower β, the further the model drifts from its initialization, the larger the KL, and generally the lower the average score. Appendix B.4 shows real RLVR outputs at β = 0.01 on constraint prompts:

Infrastructure: asynchronous RL

RLVR holds three models: policy, reference policy, and value model. The first two train; the reference only does inference. The setup:

  • ZeRO Stage 3 to fit models and optimizer states.
  • Dedicated inference GPUs — Ray allocates GPUs specifically to run vLLM's PagedAttention (adapting OpenRLHF's model allocation techniques). PagedAttention reduces memory fragmentation and redundant duplication via virtual memory and paging, letting inference run at a much larger batch size.
  • Asynchronous training — inference runs concurrently with training, cutting GPU idle time. Existing PPO frameworks are typically synchronous: inference GPUs collect rollouts, then learner GPUs train, each side idling in turn.
  • Reproducibility compensation — asynchrony introduces stale data, so the setup always trains the policy on the second-latest inference data, keeping behavior reproducible.

Evaluation Framework — The Dev / Unseen Split

Tülu 3 Eval has three design goals: reproducible, able to measure generalization to unseen tasks, and fair across a wide range of models. Its most important mechanism is a deceptively simple piece of discipline: never look at the unseen scores during development.

Why the split matters: Tülu 3's entire development process — data mixes, algorithms, hyperparameters — was steered by development scores. If only development scores were reported, you could not distinguish "the model actually got better" from "the development process overfit to these 13 benchmarks." The unseen suite was deliberately never examined during development and only brought out at the end as a ruler for overfitting. That is rare self-discipline in a technical report.

OLMES: the evaluation toolchain

The whole suite ships as allenai/olmes, built on the Eleuther AI LM Evaluation Harness. It supports flexible per-task configuration, direct access to the exact task formulations used here (and in earlier work such as OLMo and the OLMES standard), and instance-level output data for analyzing predictions and confidences. Reproducing the paper's Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct MMLU-Pro number is one line:

olmes --task mmlu_pro::tulu3 --model llama3.1-8b-instruct

Key design decisions in the development suite

  • MMLU uses zero-shot "implicit CoT." Rather than asking the model to think "step-by-step," it asks the model to "summarize" its reasoning before answering. Among the CoT settings tested, this prompt gave a systematic improvement over the standard 5-shot multiple-choice setting and maximized the number of benefiting subjects. Appendix data: on average 78% of subjects improve with implicit CoT for Tülu 3 models, while explicit CoT caps at 53%. The final metric is a macro average over subjects.
  • MATH uses flex answer extraction. Three attempts in order: (1) the Minerva format; (2) the last <ans>; (3) the text between the last two $ delimiters. The reason: even with few-shot examples, models frequently do not follow the output format. Moving from Minerva-only to flex can improve reported scores by up to 10 points.
  • Multiturn ICL. PopQA, GSM8K, MATH, BBH and others present each few-shot example as a separate conversational turn rather than stuffing them all into one prompt.
  • HumanEval samples at temperature 0.8 with pass@10. Generative evaluations cap output length at 4,096 tokens (8,192 for AlpacaEval 2).

Safety evaluation: an average over six benchmarks

Tülu 3 Safety comprises six benchmarks, all judged for refusal / compliance by the WildGuard classifier and macro-averaged: XSTest (200 unsafe plus 250 safe-but-superficially-unsafe prompts, measuring over-refusal), HarmBench (321 harmful prompts across seven semantic categories: cybercrime, chem/bio weapons, copyright, misinformation, harassment, illegal activities, general harm), Do-Anything-Now (DAN jailbreak templates × HarmBench behaviors, 300 sampled), JailbreakTrigger (13 distinct jailbreak attack methods, 400 examples), WildJailbreakTest (210 adversarial benign + 2000 adversarial harmful), and WildGuardTest (1725 items, 55% vanilla / 45% adversarial).

Design principles for the unseen suite

The unseen suite's task formulations came from a design process independent of the development suite, aiming to match how humans actually use models:

  • Formulate tasks the way humans interact with models — avoid few-shot examples presented as a dialog, and avoid precise CoT examples that tell the model how it is "supposed" to think.
  • Give clear instructions that set the context, encourage concise reasoning, and specify the final answer format.
  • Apply reasonable heuristics for answer extraction, so models aren't penalized for not following an implied syntax exactly.

The authors first validated these principles on development tasks using exploratory models predating Tülu 3, and found that being more aligned with human usage generally does not degrade performance and often lets most models do better — even after removing few-shot examples. But they deliberately did not go back and update the development task formulations, carrying the principles only into the unseen tasks. This preserves the historical comparability of development scores.

A concrete illustration (Table 29, DeepMind Mathematics): switching from base-model-adapted in-context examples to a CoT prompt moves Gemma 2 9B Inst from 18.0 → 45.9, Qwen 2.5 7B Inst from 21.2 → 54.7, and Llama 3.1 8B Inst from 20.0 → 39.4. The evaluation setup alone can be worth 20–30 points.

Two new evaluations

IFEval-OOD

52 constraints across six categories, all disjoint from IFEval's original 25. Five categories combine a verifiable constraint with an unseen WildChat prompt, human-annotated for compatibility — e.g. "mention at least 23 different person names" cannot be paired with "paraphrase a one-sentence reference containing no names." The sixth, "custom," is hand-written verifiable prompts such as CSV generation.

HREF

Human Reference-guided Evaluation of instruction Following. Covers 11 instruction-following task categories (Brainstorming, Open QA, Closed QA, Extraction, Generation, Rewriting, Summarization, Classification, Numerical Reasoning, Multi-document Synthesis, Fact Checking), with prompts and references written by professional instruction-tuning data creators.

HREF's evaluation methodology is itself a piece of research. The authors collected human judgments over responses from 16 models (four human judgments per response pair) and compared each win-rate computation setup against majority human judgment, per task category. The final setup is a composite:

  • LM judge is Llama 3.1 70B Instruct (highest agreement with humans in the pool tested); the baseline model is Llama 3.1 405B Instruct.
  • Of the 11 subtasks, all use LM-as-a-judge except Open QA and Fact Checking, which instead compute win-rate via RoBERTa-Large embedding similarity to the human references.
  • Of the remaining 9, Brainstorming and Summarization did not benefit from human references, so only the other 7 pass references to the LM judge.
  • Result: this composite procedure reaches 69.4% agreement with humans — higher than the 67% inter-human agreement.

Scaling engineering at 405B

The 405B recipe follows the 8B / 70B one closely, but ran into three classes of problems:

Compute and stability

32 nodes (256 GPUs) in parallel. Most of the codebase scaled well, but NCCL timeouts and synchronization issues required meticulous monitoring and intervention, especially in RL training. More GPUs meant more hardware failures and semi-frequent run restarts.

RLVR time budget

vLLM served inference with 16-way tensor parallelism; the remaining 240 GPUs trained, with weights synced via NCCL broadcast after each update. Per iteration: inference ~550s, weight transfer ~25s, training ~1,500s. To cut cost, the value model was only 8B.

Limited tuning

Compute cost made hyperparameter tuning largely infeasible. Following prior Tülu and Llama practice, they lowered the learning rate for the larger model, training with a "lighter touch" (SFT 2e-6, DPO 2e-7, RLVR 1e-7).

The 405B RLVR data is different. Because SFT and DPO alone already saturated GSM8K, the GSM8K data was removed entirely; initial runs also showed IFEval data did not help much, so 405B RLVR trained on the MATH train set only. Surprisingly, even 25 RLVR steps improved MATH by over 5 points, and it kept climbing. Compute constraints capped training at 75 steps — fewer than the smaller models — and the authors state plainly that MATH performance had not saturated in either training or testing, so further training should help. They also suggest future work explore larger value models or value-model-free RL algorithms such as GRPO.

SECTION 09

What Didn't Work, Limitations, and Future Work

The paper has an entire section titled "Insights from the Unfruitful." In an environment where technical reports typically publish only good news, its information density may exceed that of the main results.

Two methods tried but left out of the final recipe

Online DPO — no improvement, and MATH got worse.

Standard DPO is offline: preference data is collected ahead of time, so the policy never gets feedback on its own generations during training. Online DPO (Guo et al., 2024) mitigates this distributional shift in three steps: (1) sample 2 responses to a prompt from the current policy; (2) obtain online feedback on the pair; (3) update the policy with standard DPO loss on that pairwise data. The original paper uses online AI feedback for step 2; Tülu 3 substituted a trained RM to scale experiments.

Both targets were tried: for general capability, an RM trained one epoch on Skywork's 82K preference points; for math reasoning, that same RM continued on the authors' synthetic on-policy math preference data. The result — training on top of a Tülu 3 DPO checkpoint for 200K episodes on math problems — was no or little improvement on GSM8K and degradation on MATH, across various sampling temperatures and KL penalty coefficients. They did not pursue it further, and suggest future work investigate different sampling methods or RM architectures.

Rejection sampling — the gains weren't worth the compute.

The procedure: train an initial model on an SFT + preference mix, use it to generate n responses per SFT prompt, rank those (plus the original response) with an RM or LLM judge, keep the best; the rest can form chosen/rejected pairs for preference optimization. Rerun the whole post-training pipeline on the new data and repeat until performance converges.

Tülu 3 tried it and concluded that in their setup the gains were minimal for the compute required, leaving deeper exploration to future work. Two qualitative findings worth recording: (1) strong judges are vital — publicly available models often struggle to pick the best of the candidates; (2) including the original response among the judge's options (choosing the best out of the n generations plus the original) performed much better than choosing only among new generations.

Four limitations and directions the authors name

Long context and multi-turn

Tülu 3's data is relatively short and contains no long multi-turn conversations — the average is 2.4 turns and the majority of samples are under 2,048 tokens. Longer context enables new use cases and more in-context examples; a non-trivial share of real user conversations exceed 2 turns. Both need dedicated training and evaluation.

Multilinguality

Tülu 3 focuses on English data and evaluations (the multilingual Aya dataset is included only for its quality). The authors note multilingual post-training may need different techniques — cross-lingual alignment, or careful data balancing strategies — making it an interesting and impactful direction.

Tool use and agents

Tülu 3 is evaluated on its own, but LMs are increasingly deployed inside larger systems with tools or agent frameworks. The authors specifically note that training models to use tools is a natural way to dramatically improve reasoning and math, rather than trying to accomplish everything "in the weights."

More complex verifiers

RLVR currently covers two domains (math, exact instruction following) and three evaluations, all with relatively straightforward verification. More complex verifiers are explicitly left to future work — a footnote points to recent success using code execution feedback for RL.

In retrospect: what Tülu 3 actually changed

Tülu 3's significance is not in any single score. Its 8B doesn't even win on average against Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct, and trails it by more than 25 points on MATH.

Its significance is that for the first time, someone wrote down the complete modern post-training pipeline — every data mixing ablation, every hyperparameter, the methods that failed, and the actual contamination rates of public datasets — and shipped it with the weights, data, and code. RLVR matters in its own right too: it demonstrates that RL on verifiable binary signals can be integrated into a generalist pipeline rather than just improving math in isolation, while honestly showing both its ceiling (near-zero gain at 70B) and its failure mode (too small a β turns the model into a verifier parasite).

After this, "the open community can't do good post-training" can no longer be explained by "nobody knows how."
